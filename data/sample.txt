The workshop opens when the morning light reaches the window.
Mira puts a notebook on the table and writes a question on the first page.
How does a machine learn to tell a story?
It begins with a sequence of symbols and a prediction about what comes next.
A letter follows another letter. A word follows another word.
Each small prediction becomes a lesson, and each lesson changes the model.

At the next table, Leo builds a tiny language model from simple parts.
First he turns text into numbers. Each byte receives its own number.
The numbers become vectors, and the vectors carry information through the network.
Position vectors help the model distinguish the beginning from the end.
Attention lets each position look at the positions that came before it.
The future stays hidden, because the answer must be predicted rather than copied.

Mira reads the first generated sentence aloud and laughs.
The model has invented a word that no one has heard before.
Leo checks the loss and runs another batch of examples.
A gradient points toward a small improvement. The optimizer takes a small step.
They repeat the process, watching a line of numbers become a pattern.
Learning is a long conversation between a prediction and a correction.

Outside the workshop, rain begins to tap against the glass.
The garden smells of leaves and wet soil. A bird waits beneath a branch.
Inside, the little model tries to describe the rain.
It knows the shape of a sentence before it knows what a sentence means.
It learns that a question often ends with a question mark.
It learns that a story can begin with a person, a place, and a change.

The next morning brings a clear sky and another experiment.
Mira changes the number of attention heads and records the result.
Leo makes the context longer so that more of the story fits in one window.
They keep a separate piece of text to check what the model has learned.
A lower training loss is useful, but unfamiliar examples tell a different story.
An experiment is most useful when another person can repeat it.
